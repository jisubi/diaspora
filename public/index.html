A century away from home

They were sent away.
They were never brought back.

In 1905, over a thousand Korean families boarded a ship for Mexico believing they were going to work. They arrived as bonded laborers on henequen plantations. Their great-grandchildren are still there — and most have never seen Korea.

Diaspora — Missions to the Forgotten Korean Communities of Latin America

Halmoni, 87

She still sets the table for people who never came home.

Her grandfather signed a contract he could not read. Four years of cutting henequen under the Yucatán sun became a lifetime — the ships home never sailed, the wages never came, and the war made Korea a country you could no longer return to.

Today she lives in two rooms with a tin roof. She still folds mandu on New Year's, the way her mother taught her, in a village where no one else speaks a word of Korean.

She has never asked anyone for anything. We are asking on her behalf.

1905 — Today

A hundred and twenty years. Four generations. One unbroken silence.

What began as a labor contract became an exile. These families were scattered across Mexico, Cuba, Guatemala and Brazil — and as Korea rebuilt itself into one of the world's great economies, the descendants of those first ships were left out of the story entirely.

1905

1,033 Koreans sail from Jemulpo to Yucatán on the Ilford, promised paid work.

1921

Hundreds move on to Cuba's sugar fields after the henequen market collapses.

1950

War closes the door home. Return becomes impossible; the language begins to fade.

Now

An estimated 30,000 descendants remain — many in deep poverty, most without documents.
